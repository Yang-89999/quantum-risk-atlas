How the model works and where it is rough
  • Logical layer. RSA: Gidney–Ekerå windowed arithmetic (≈3n logical qubits, 0.3n³ Toffolis at n=2048) or Gidney 2025 approximate residue arithmetic (≈1,400 logical qubits, 6.5·10⁹ Toffolis), scaled as n³ for other sizes. ECC: Roetteler et al. (9n + 2⌈log₂n⌉ + 10 qubits, 448n³log₂n + 4090n³ Toffolis). Finite-field DLP: same modular arithmetic as RSA with the exponent length set by the variant (full-length for safe-prime groups; ≈3× the security level for Ekerå–Håstad short exponents). AES: Grassl et al. logical qubits and per-iteration T-depth with ⌈π/4·√(2ⁿ/P)⌉ Grover iterations.
  • Fault-tolerance layer. Logical error per cycle p_L = 0.1·(p/p_th)^((d+1)/2) with p_th = 1%. The distance is the smallest odd d such that L × cycles × p_L stays below the allowed failure probability. Physical qubits = L·2d²·storage·routing + factories × footprint·d² (footprint 300d² for distillation, 30d² for cultivation). Runtime = T × max(reaction time / parallelism, 10·d·t_cycle / factories).
  • Settings II–IV (placeholder). One operation is currently derived from the complete attack: a controlled multiplication or point addition is 1/(2n) of the attack's Toffoli count, a window-selected one is w times that (w = 5 for modular arithmetic, 8 for curves), a fixed multiplication 0.9 of a controlled one; one Grover iteration is total T-depth / iterations, the oracle 97% of an iteration, and one reversible AES evaluation the oracle divided by 2 × (plaintext–ciphertext pairs). These ratios are to be replaced by the paper-specific counts.
  • Known gaps. No modelling of layout-specific routing costs, decoder throughput limits, or non-surface codes (qLDPC). Grover numbers ignore the MAXDEPTH limit that NIST imposes; with it, AES-128 is already out of reach. Treat all outputs as order-of-magnitude.

The Quantum-Safe Readiness Index

IBM's Institute for Business Value defined the Quantum-Safe Readiness Index (QSRI) in 2023 to assess, and re-assess over time, the readiness of an organisation, an industry or a region. It evaluates 14 activities, or indicators, grouped into three areas, quantum-safe discovery, observability and transformation, weights them according to IBM's subject-matter expertise and client experience, and reports the result on a 100-point scale. In the 2025 study of 750 executives the average organisation scored 25, up from 21 in 2023.

The index on this page keeps that structure: three categories, 14 indicators, 100 points. Each indicator i is rated on a maturity scale si from 0 (not started) to 4 (operational). A category score is the weighted mean of its indicators, and the index is the weighted mean of the three category scores:

Cc = 100 × Σi∈c wi si4 × Σi∈c wiIndex = Σc Wc CcΣc Wc

IBM does not publish its indicator list with weights, so the indicator wording, the indicator weights wi (1.5 for cryptographic inventory, crypto-agility and PQC in production, 1 otherwise) and the category weights Wc (discovery 1, observability 1, transformation 1.5) are this project's own choices. They are shown beside each item and can be replaced one for one with IBM's indicators from the report figure. With the ratings currently set, . The IBM 2025 average is the benchmark line in the chart, so a client's number reads on the same 100-point scale, with the caveat that the weights differ. The bands are Exposed (below 25), Starting (25 to 49), Progressing (50 to 74) and Quantum-safe track (75 and above). Unlike the survey index, this one is computed live from the client's ratings and is tested against the Q-day estimate from section 01 through Mosca's inequality.

PQC Benchmark Suite

Explore cryptographic schemes, known attacks, and reproducible resource-estimation benchmarks.

The suite organises four things that are usually scattered: what is known about attacks on each post-quantum scheme, the assumptions behind every resource estimate, the benchmark cases that pin a scheme, parameter set and attack to a cost model, and the evidence that a reported number can be reproduced. It is a project-level research framework, not a standard, and nothing in it is endorsed by NIST.

Quantum Lab: AI agents for cryptographic security research

A proposed system of coordinated AI agents that helps researchers investigate cryptographic risk in the quantum era: the quantum threat to today's cryptography, the security of post-quantum schemes, and the defences and migration that follow.

Mission Proposed

Cryptographic security questions in the quantum era arrive faster than they can be answered one paper at a time: which deployed systems a quantum computer could threaten first, whether the assumptions behind a post-quantum scheme hold up, and whether a chosen defence removes a risk or only moves it. The Quantum Lab proposes a team of AI agents that works alongside researchers to investigate those risks, test security assumptions and evaluate potential defences, with every experiment reproducible and every significant conclusion reviewed by a person.

  • Investigate risks. Map which systems a quantum computer, or a new classical attack, could threaten, and under which assumptions.
  • Test assumptions. Turn a security assumption into an experiment on a public toy example or a sandboxed replica, and run it.
  • Evaluate defences. Check whether a proposed mitigation or migration choice removes the risk, reduces it, or moves it elsewhere.
Not an operational platform. The roles, workflow and tools on this page are a design. No agent is deployed and no backend exists.
Not a proof of security. An AI assessment can produce evidence against a system. It cannot, by itself, show that a system is secure.
Only authorised targets. Future tests run on public toy examples, sandboxed replicas, or targets with explicit written authorisation.
Two questions, kept apart. Classical cryptanalysis of a post-quantum algorithm is a different question from estimating the quantum resources an attack on RSA or ECC would need. Results are labelled with which question they answer.

The agent teams Proposed

Five roles, three of them AI agents, one an independent checking agent, and one a person. Each hands its output to the next, and the human expert decides what any of it means. These are proposed roles; none is running.

  2. Threat model
  3. Red-team test
  4. Blue-team response
  5. Independent evaluation
  6. Expert review
  7. Retest ↺ returns to step 3 when results are inconclusive or a defence changed

Reports two agent-driven results, each verified with computational experiments and published code or a paper: a faster enumeration attack on HAWK, a candidate in NIST's additional-signatures process, exploiting a symmetry of its lattice; and a chosen-plaintext meet-in-the-middle attack on a 7-round version of AES-128 (full AES-128 has 10 rounds). The post states that neither result affects production systems and that the AES result does not break the full cipher.
