Quantum Lab: AI agents for cryptographic security research

A proposed system of coordinated AI agents that helps researchers investigate cryptographic risk in the quantum era: the quantum threat to today's cryptography, the security of post-quantum schemes, and the defences and migration that follow.

Mission Proposed

Cryptographic security questions in the quantum era arrive faster than they can be answered one paper at a time: which deployed systems a quantum computer could threaten first, whether the assumptions behind a post-quantum scheme hold up, and whether a chosen defence removes a risk or only moves it. The Quantum Lab proposes a team of AI agents that works alongside researchers to investigate those risks, test security assumptions and evaluate potential defences, with every experiment reproducible and every significant conclusion reviewed by a person.

  • Investigate risks. Map which systems a quantum computer, or a new classical attack, could threaten, and under which assumptions.
  • Test assumptions. Turn a security assumption into an experiment on a public toy example or a sandboxed replica, and run it.
  • Evaluate defences. Check whether a proposed mitigation or migration choice removes the risk, reduces it, or moves it elsewhere.
Not an operational platform. The roles, workflow and tools on this page are a design. No agent is deployed and no backend exists.
Not a proof of security. An AI assessment can produce evidence against a system. It cannot, by itself, show that a system is secure.
Only authorised targets. Future tests run on public toy examples, sandboxed replicas, or targets with explicit written authorisation.
Two questions, kept apart. Classical cryptanalysis of a post-quantum algorithm is a different question from estimating the quantum resources an attack on RSA or ECC would need. Results are labelled with which question they answer.

The agent teams Proposed

Five roles, three of them AI agents, one an independent checking agent, and one a person. Each hands its output to the next, and the human expert decides what any of it means. These are proposed roles; none is running.

Proposed role · agent

Planner

Defines the research question, the target, the threat model, the scope and the evaluation criteria before any test begins.

Hands over: a written research brief
Proposed role · agent

Red team

Proposes potential attacks or weaknesses and tests them on the authorised target, recording code, parameters and outcomes.

Hands over: hypotheses, test results, evidence
Proposed role · agent

Blue team

Analyses the red team's findings and investigates defensive responses, including their cost and what they leave uncovered.

Hands over: analysis, candidate defences
Proposed role · checking agent

Independent evaluator

Re-checks both teams' methods, calculations and evidence, reproducing results where it can and flagging what it cannot.

Hands over: reproduction status, verdict on evidence
Proposed role · person

Human expert

Reviews significant findings and determines what conclusions are justified, what is published, and what goes back for a retest.

Decides: conclusions, publication, retest

Research workflow Proposed

  1. Research question
  2. Threat model
  3. Red-team test
  4. Blue-team response
  5. Independent evaluation
  6. Expert review
  7. Retest ↺ returns to step 3 when results are inconclusive or a defence changed

Quantum threat assessment

Estimating the quantum resources an attack on existing cryptography would need, and when hardware might supply them. This is a question about a future quantum computer, not about the mathematics of the scheme.

References Published

  1. Anthropic Frontier Red Team, “Discovering cryptographic weaknesses with Claude”, July 2026. Reports two agent-driven results, each verified with computational experiments and published code or a paper: a faster enumeration attack on HAWK, a candidate in NIST's additional-signatures process, exploiting a symmetry of its lattice; and a chosen-plaintext meet-in-the-middle attack on a 7-round version of AES-128 (full AES-128 has 10 rounds). The post states that neither result affects production systems and that the AES result does not break the full cipher. Cited here as inspiration for the pattern of agents, computational experiments and verification; it is not evidence that the system proposed on this page works.
  2. NIST, FIPS 203, Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism Standard (ML-KEM), August 2024. The standard named in the example case above.
  3. NIST, Post-Quantum Cryptography: additional digital signature schemes. The process in which HAWK is a candidate.
